Development board

The board you already use. Cards stay in their columns; a card that decomposes grows one extra line, and opening that line lists what it breaks into.

What this variant argues

Thesis

Nothing moves. The column board is the view; decomposition is a disclosure inside a card, so nobody has to learn a second surface to see a tree that was always in the data.

The expansion is an index, not a relocation

A sub-row is a reference to a card, never the card itself. Every card still renders exactly once, in its own column, in the order the board already puts it. Opening a parent lists its children as compact rows that point at where those cards actually live, and each child card carries a matching under line back to its parent.

The cross-column child

theme-contract-for-plugin-surfaces sits in Backlog while its parent plugin-system-unification sits in In review. Because a sub-row is a reference, this is not a contradiction to resolve — it is a fact to state. Every sub-row carries the column its card is in, and shows it exactly when the answer is not the column you are reading: hairline outline, an arrow, and the column name spelled out. Pressing it scrolls the real card into view and flashes it. Same-column rows keep that label for screen readers and spend the pixels on their title instead. Nesting never overrides status; status wins, and nesting annotates.

Tree filtering

The parent stays as context. A card survives the filter if it matches on title or id, or if anything below it matches. Cards kept only for context are drawn in muted ink so the true matches stay black, they auto-expand, and their disclosure line switches to reading 3 of 10 subtasks match. Nothing is promoted to a root it does not belong to, because promoting a child would move a card, and moving cards is the one thing this variant refuses to do. Column counts drop their WIP limit while a filter is on, exactly as the shipped board does, since a filtered count is not a WIP count.

What this is bad at

Depth. Two levels read fine; a five-deep tree turns one card into a tall block that pushes everything under it far down its column while the columns beside it stay short. The board's steadiest quality, four stacks scanning at the same rhythm, is the first thing the expansion spends. Indentation also runs out of room fast in a column roughly 300px wide, so past three levels the sub-rows truncate to a few words and the depth stops being legible. And because a sub-row is a reference, a same-column child is on screen twice: once as a row inside its parent and once as its own card lower down. That is the honest cost of never moving a card. If the owner's real ambition is reading a decomposition top to bottom, this variant makes you assemble that reading yourself, one expansion at a time.
